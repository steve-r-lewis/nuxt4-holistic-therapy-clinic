# Three Muscles That Secretly Run Your Posture

After nearly twenty-six years as a massage therapist, I've learned that the body is full of surprises. Some muscles get all the attention—everyone knows about their biceps or their "lats" from the gym. But these two muscles in particular quietly cause more trouble than most people realise: the biceps brachii (brachii meaning "of the arm"), latissimus dorsi—the big back muscle that gives swimmers and climbers that V-shaped torso. In addition to this troublesome duo is the lesser-known coracobrachialis (try saying that three times fast). These muscles don't always hurt when they're unhappy. Instead, they pull your body out of alignment in ways that create problems elsewhere.

## How a Tight Shoulder Becomes a Sore Neck

Your shoulder is more complex than just the ball-and-socket joint you can see. Deep underneath, these three muscles anchor to your shoulder blade and upper arm bone. When they get tight or irritated—whether from desk work, workouts, or old injuries—they tend to pull your arm inward and roll your shoulder forward.

Once your shoulder starts creeping forward, your neck tries to adjust to the misalignment. Your head juts forward like a chicken pecking at the ground, and the muscles in your upper back and neck strain to pull everything back into place. Over time, they get exhausted, knotted, and painful. That's why so many people with shoulder issues also complain of neck and upper back pain; the muscles there are working overtime to compensate.

## The Muscle Nobody Talks About: Latissimus Dorsi

</p>

Lately, I've become fascinated with the latissimus dorsi, or "lat" for short. Most people think of it as a shoulder or back muscle, and it is.  Think of the lat like a big elastic band running from your lower back all the way to your upper arm. When it's tight, it doesn't just affect your shoulder. It tugs on your lower back. It can tilt your pelvis. It can even throw off your walking pattern because it's linked through your back to your opposite buttock muscle.

Lower back pain is often connected to tight hamstrings, hip flexors, and glutes. But a tight latissimus dorsi is an overlooked culprit in many cases. When this muscle is restricted, it can:

* Arch your lower back excessively  
* Tilt your pelvis forward  
* Shut down your glute muscles, forcing your lower back to work harder  
* Restrict your ribcage, making you breathe more with your neck and shoulder muscles instead of your diaphragm

That last point is worth repeating: a tight lat can literally change how you breathe. And when you breathe poorly, your neck muscles pick up the slack. So a problem that started in your shoulder or lower back can end up giving you a tension headache.

### The Body Is One Piece

What I love about this work is that the body keeps teaching me new connections. You might come in suffering from neck pain, but the real issue is your shoulder. You might come in with lower back pain, but your latissimus dorsi is the hidden troublemaker.

Your body is more connected than it looks on an anatomy chart. That nagging pain in one spot might be coming from somewhere completely unexpected. And sometimes, the muscles that cause the most trouble are the ones you never knew you had.

If there's one thing hands-on work has taught me, it's this: pain is rarely where the story begins. It's where the story ends up.

You don't need to memorise Latin names or become your own anatomy chart. But the next time your neck aches after a long day at the desk, or your lower back flares up for no obvious reason, consider looking upstream. Is your shoulder rolled forward? Is your breathing shallow? Is there a muscle you've never heard of quietly pulling everything out of line?

The body doesn't think in isolated parts, even if our minds do. Treating the neck without checking the shoulder, or the back without considering the lat, is like silencing a smoke alarm without looking for the fire.

So be curious about your own body. Notice your posture. Notice your breathing. And if you're dealing with persistent pain that doesn't seem to go away, remember: the culprit might be hiding in plain sight, or in a muscle you can't even pronounce.
